Interplanetary Survival Guide: Martian Map

Navigation for the first crews driving on Mars.

Mars Navigator is a Google-Maps-style app for astronauts driving a rover in Gale Crater. It brings real NASA orbital and rover data together on one map, plans routes that stay on safe slopes and away from hazards, and keeps working when Earth is too far away to help.

The problem

On Mars, nobody can tell you which way to turn.

Rovers have been driven from Earth for decades, with teams planning each move a day at a time. A crew on the surface has to make those calls on the spot, about terrain that has already stopped rovers.

Light delay to Earth
4–24 min

One way, depending on where the planets are. A question and its answer can take most of an hour, so crews need answers on board.

Spirit, 2009
Stuck

NASA's Spirit rover bogged down in soft soil and never drove free. Hidden terrain, not distance, is what ends a traverse.

Curiosity, 2013
Re-routed

Sharp rocks punctured Curiosity's wheels, and drivers changed its routes to protect them. Terrain-aware routing is how you avoid that damage.

Key features and their impact

Everything a crew needs to plan a safe traverse.

Each feature runs on real Gale Crater data in the demo. Use the links to open the demo on that feature.

01 · Hazard-aware routing

Two routes on real terrain: direct, or safe.

The router plans across the stereo terrain model with a grade limit for the rover, steers around mapped hazard zones, and shows the elevation profile of each route. If no route is safe, it says so instead of drawing one anyway.

Impact

Keeps crews off slopes and ground that can roll or trap a rover, before they commit to a drive.

Try it in the demo →
02 · Multi-stop expeditions

Plan the whole sol, then drive it.

Chain science stops into one trip, reorder them, and simulate the drive. Speed changes with the grade, so the ETA, time per leg and grade profile reflect the actual ground. Tap the map to add a via-point and the route re-plans around hazards.

Impact

Realistic timelines for EVAs and drives, so crews can budget power, consumables and daylight before they leave.

Try it in the demo →
03 · Temperature through the sol

Ground and air temperature, hour by hour, in five seasons.

Our model runs soil physics on THEMIS thermal inertia and TES albedo for every pixel, corrected against Curiosity's weather station. Scrub the slider to see the crater heat and cool through a whole sol. The app shows the model's limits next to the map.

Impact

Helps crews time EVAs and drives around the coldest and hottest hours, which matters for suits, batteries and instruments.

Try it in the demo →
04 · Dust and telemetry

Dust opacity, dust devils and slope at a glance.

The Home card shows modelled ground temperature, dust opacity τ from Curiosity's Mastcam record, and the slope under the rover. A dust-devil heating index flags where the ground heats most strongly, and an advisory warns when a hot spot is close.

Impact

Dust cuts solar power and visibility. Seeing it early lets crews plan around it instead of reacting to it.

Try it in the demo →
05 · Street Level

Stand where Curiosity stood.

Five real Curiosity panoramas, placed on the map at the spots where they were taken. Look around in 360° and follow the arrows to walk between them.

Impact

Crews can see a site at ground level before they arrive. The same views work for training and for showing the public what Gale Crater looks like.

Try it in the demo →
06 · Route assistant

Ask in plain words, get a route you can load.

"Plan a 3-stop science day that stays clear of dust devils." A Claude-powered assistant answers from the app's own landmarks, hazards and telemetry and proposes a route you can load with one tap. When the model can't be reached, a built-in planner answers from the same data.

Impact

A second opinion on the surface, without waiting up to an hour for Earth. It keeps answering even with no connection.

Try it in the demo →

What we checked, and what we didn't
  • Ground temperature is within about 5 K of Curiosity's readings at the rover, and air temperature within about 4 K, on data held back from the fit.
  • Accuracy away from the rover is not measured: every REMS reading comes from an area of about 10 × 5 km. The app says this next to the heat map.
  • The dust-devil layer is a heating index, not a probability.
  • Every data source is credited, with links, on the app's About screen.
